# Stagebound --- Story & World Reference

> **Working concept:** A gacha-based, turn-based/idle RPG set inside an
> impossible interdimensional theater. The player serves as
> **Co-Director**, working alongside a powerful, chaotic cosmic being
> known as **The Director** to assemble a Cast from countless realms and
> put on increasingly dangerous Productions.

## 1. Core Premise

Somewhere between worlds exists an impossible place known as **The Grand
Theater**.

It belongs to no single realm, kingdom, age, or reality. Its stage can
become a ruined castle one night, an enchanted forest the next, and
something entirely alien after that. Its doors can reach places that
should never be connected.

Presiding over it is an ancient supernatural being known simply as **The
Director**.

The Director is immensely powerful, theatrical, impulsive, witty, and
dangerously fond of spectacle. He can reach across realities, summon
legendary warriors and strange creatures, reconstruct places and events
as elaborate Productions, and bend many of the Theater's rules.

Unfortunately, raw cosmic power has not made him particularly good at
making sensible creative decisions.

That is where the player comes in.

At the beginning of the game, the player is unexpectedly brought to the
Grand Theater and offered a position:

**Co-Director of the Grand Theater.**

The Director provides the impossible magic and spectacle. The player
provides the judgment.

Together, they assemble a **Cast** of people and creatures drawn from
countless realms and direct them through battles presented as
performances before the Theater's mysterious Audience.

On the surface, The Director claims the entire endeavor exists for a
simple reason:

**Eternity is boring, and he intends to put on the greatest show
existence has ever seen.**

Whether that is the complete truth is another matter.

## 2. The Player --- The Co-Director

The player is **not** The Director and does not secretly control him.

The player exists within the fiction as the Grand Theater's
**Co-Director**. The Co-Director does not need a canonical appearance,
gender, voice, or detailed backstory.

### Division of Responsibility

**The Director** - Powers the Grand Theater. - Opens passages between
realms. - Performs the supernatural act of summoning. - Creates and
alters Productions. - Introduces battles and major events. - Frequently
causes problems in the name of improving the show.

**The Co-Director** - Chooses the Cast. - Builds parties. - Directs
combat decisions. - Selects abilities and interacts with combat
systems. - Manages Performers. - Organizes the Audience. - Develops and
upgrades the Theater. - Makes the practical decisions required to keep
the production alive.

> **The Director has the power. The Co-Director has the judgment.**

They are partners rather than master and servant.

## 3. The Director

**Title:** The Director\
**True Name:** Unknown\
**Nature:** Unknown cosmic/supernatural entity\
**Role:** Host, guide, narrator, troublemaker, and major story character

The Director should be one of the game's most recognizable
personalities.

### Personality

-   Charismatic
-   Witty
-   Dramatic
-   Mischievous
-   Extremely powerful
-   Easily bored
-   Enthusiastic about spectacle
-   Occasionally irresponsible
-   Surprisingly fond of the Cast
-   Capable of becoming genuinely serious

He should not be written as a conventional evil god.

He may kidnap---or, as he prefers to say, **"professionally
recruit"**---people from across reality, but he does not view himself as
cruel. He genuinely enjoys seeing people overcome impossible
circumstances and becomes invested in good Performers.

His humor often comes from the contrast between cosmic power and
questionable judgment.

### Example Voice

> "Infinite worlds. Legendary warriors. Unspeakable monsters. Tragedy.
> Romance. Heroism. Occasional dismemberment. Everything a respectable
> production requires."

> "Excellent. Exactly as planned."

> "...Don't write that down."

> "I didn't kidnap anyone. We are conducting an extremely aggressive
> casting process."

> "Co-Director, I have improved tonight's performance."

That last sentence should usually be concerning.

### Writing Rule

The Director should be funny, but **not merely comic relief**. When the
jokes stop, it should matter.

## 4. Opening Setup

The game begins in darkness.

A stage light switches on. The Director is standing beneath it.

> "Ah. You're awake."

> "Good."

> "I imagine you're wondering where you are."

> "More importantly, you're probably wondering *why* you're here."

> "Long story."

> "Short version: I require a Co-Director."

The darkness beyond him illuminates, revealing the Grand Theater.

> "Welcome to the Grand Theater."

> "Infinite worlds. Legendary warriors. Unspeakable monsters. Tragedy.
> Romance. Heroism. Occasional dismemberment."

> "Everything a respectable production requires."

He glances toward evidence of one of his previous disastrous attempts at
running the Theater alone.

> "Unfortunately, my previous productions have suffered from what the
> critics insist on calling..."

> "*catastrophic creative decisions.*"

He turns back toward the player.

> "That's where you come in."

This sequence leads naturally into the tutorial.

## 5. The Grand Theater

The Grand Theater exists **between realities**. Its true origin is
unknown.

It contains far more than a single stage:

-   Main Stage
-   Audience Hall
-   Backstage
-   Dressing Rooms
-   Prop Room
-   Casting Stage
-   Director's Box
-   Workshop
-   Storage areas
-   Restricted wings
-   Sealed rooms
-   Spaces that should not physically fit inside the building

The Theater should feel simultaneously cozy, whimsical, ancient, and
slightly impossible.

### Visual Progression

The Theater begins modestly: worn floors, simple curtains, cheap
seating, limited lighting, empty audience sections, and basic scenery.

As the player progresses, the Theater physically improves through better
seating, additional rows, balconies, royal boxes, improved curtains,
chandeliers, magical stage equipment, ornate architecture, and expanded
backstage facilities.

The player's progression should be visible in the Theater itself.

## 6. The Cast

The player's collection of playable characters is:

# **The Cast**

An individual playable character is a:

# **Performer**

"Actor" can be used conversationally, particularly by The Director, but
**Performer** is the preferred general term.

Performers can come from virtually anywhere: knights, mages,
mercenaries, monsters, kings, thieves, scholars, constructs, talking
animals, spirits, legendary heroes, or ordinary people in extraordinary
circumstances.

An armored bear is entirely appropriate.

### Performer Motivation

Performers do not necessarily appreciate being summoned.

Some want to return home. Some enjoy the Theater. Some want power or
answers. Some have nowhere to return to. Others see the Theater as a
second chance.

The Director promises that a Performer who reaches the end of the
ultimate Production may receive something extraordinary---potentially
even a **wish** capable of changing their fate.

Whether he can actually fulfill that promise remains unresolved.

## 7. Casting --- The Gacha System

Character summoning is represented in-world as **Casting**.

The Co-Director chooses to seek new talent. The Director performs the
supernatural summoning.

A Casting sequence can take place on a dark stage using spotlights,
curtains, portals, magical effects, dice, and theatrical reveals.

The Director can provide randomized commentary.

After a poor pull:

> "You requested a legendary warrior."

A confused farmer appears.

> "Look at his shoulders."

For a rare pull, a portal violently erupts and a legendary warrior steps
through.

> "...I meant to do that."

### Duplicates

Duplicate pulls are themed as:

# **Encores**

Possible terminology includes **Encore**, **Encore Rank**, and **Encore
Shards**. This represents further investment in an existing Performer
rather than literally creating copies of the same person.

## 8. Productions, Acts, and Scenes

Campaign structure follows theatrical terminology.

-   **Production** --- major campaign/story
-   **Act** --- chapter within a Production
-   **Scene** --- individual stage/battle

Example:

> **THE FALL OF ARDENDALE**\
> Act II --- The Siege\
> Scene 7 --- The Broken Gates

Use this instead of generic labels such as "World 3-17" wherever
practical.

## 9. Productions In-Universe

The Grand Theater can reconstruct places, events, memories, legends, and
perhaps unrealized possibilities from connected realms.

Battles are theatrical recreations.

The Theater can construct fantasy kingdoms, forests, volcanoes, frozen
wastes, haunted ruins, magical academies, pirate worlds, clockwork
cities, celestial landscapes, and alien realms.

Environments should remain visibly theatrical through painted backdrops,
stage flats, mechanical props, visible rigging, hanging moons,
artificial weather, trapdoors, stage lights, and moving scenery.

**Embrace the artificiality.**

A dragon can genuinely be lowered onto the stage by ropes.

## 10. Combat as Performance

Battles are thematically **Performances**.

The Co-Director makes the important tactical decisions for the Cast.

The exact combat system is still being prototyped, but the narrative
framework should support:

-   Turn-based combat
-   Dice-driven ability mechanics
-   Player-selected actions
-   Automated/idle combat
-   Character ultimates
-   Audience interaction
-   Boss battles

### Dice

Dice are expected to be an important combat mechanic, but their exact
implementation is intentionally **not locked**.

Possible uses include determining available abilities, assigning dice to
Performers, triggering special effects, activating combinations, or
allowing characters and Audience members to manipulate chance.

## 11. The Audience

The Audience is both part of the world and a gameplay progression
system.

Audience members can be collected and placed into available seats.
Different members provide bonuses to the Cast.

Examples:

-   **Retired Knight** --- improves Warrior damage.
-   **Fortune Teller** --- manipulates dice outcomes.
-   **Wealthy Noble** --- increases earned currency.
-   **Excitable Child** --- increases Hype from critical hits.
-   **Former Boss** --- grants a unique bonus associated with that boss.

Audience members may come from story progression, boss victories,
achievements, events, Theater upgrades, hidden objectives, or reward
systems.

Defeated enemies and bosses can eventually become spectators. Use this
for both humor and worldbuilding.

## 12. Audience Hype

Performances may include an **Audience Hype** system.

Critical hits, high rolls, combos, ultimates, kills, boss staggers, and
dramatic defensive plays can increase Hype. Poor performances can reduce
it.

Possible states:

**Quiet → Interested → Excited → Roaring → Standing Ovation**

Higher Hype can provide combat bonuses, and Audience members can modify
its generation and effects.

This remains subject to gameplay prototyping.

## 13. Spotlight / Ultimate Abilities

Character ultimates should lean heavily into theatrical presentation.

When a Performer activates an ultimate:

1.  Stage lighting dims.
2.  A spotlight isolates the Performer.
3.  Other characters become visually subdued.
4.  The Performer's name/title appears.
5.  The Performer executes an exaggerated signature move.
6.  The Audience reacts.

Possible names include **Spotlight**, **Showstopper**, or **Finale**.

Final terminology is TBD.

## 14. The Director's Box

The Theater contains a **Director's Box** overlooking the stage.

It has two positions:

-   The Director's seat
-   The Co-Director's seat

The Co-Director's seat should generally remain visually empty.

The implication is that **the player is sitting there**.

This gives the player a physical presence without requiring a canonical
avatar.

## 15. The Stage Manager

A future major supporting character should be considered:

# **The Stage Manager**

The Stage Manager is the practical counterweight to The Director.

Where The Director is chaotic, the Stage Manager is organized. Where he
wants spectacle, the Stage Manager worries about whether the stage is
currently on fire.

Possible roles include tutorial assistance, Theater upgrades, system
explanations, event management, quest administration, story exposition,
and comic straight-man.

Example:

> **Director:** "We need lava."

> **Stage Manager:** "No."

> **Director:** "A little lava."

> **Stage Manager:** "No."

> **Director:** "Co-Director?"

The recurring trio becomes:

**The Director --- Power and chaos**\
**The Co-Director --- Strategy and leadership**\
**The Stage Manager --- Organization and reason**

## 16. Tone

The game should be funny, charming, adventurous, high-fantasy,
occasionally absurd, character-driven, and capable of becoming genuinely
dramatic.

It should **not** be a parody where nothing matters.

Characters can be ridiculous while still having sincere stories. The
armored bear can be funny simply because he is an armored bear while
still having history, motivations, relationships, and heroic moments.

> **A world that doesn't take itself too seriously, populated by
> characters whose stories still matter.**

## 17. The Long-Term Mystery

The initial explanation is intentionally simple: The Director operates
the Grand Theater because eternity became boring and he wanted
entertainment.

Over time, inconsistencies appear.

Questions include:

-   Where did the Grand Theater actually come from?
-   Why can it reach other realities?
-   Why were these particular Performers selected?
-   Who are the oldest members of the Audience?
-   Why do some Audience members know things they shouldn't?
-   What exists in the Theater's sealed sections?
-   Why are some seats permanently empty?
-   Why are there realms The Director refuses to visit?
-   What is The Director's true identity?
-   Why was the player selected as Co-Director?

### Potential Major Revelation

A strong future direction:

**The Director did not create the Grand Theater.**

He found it.

The Theater is older than he is---or at least older than he admits. The
Director may understand it far less than he pretends.

An even later possibility:

The Audience may not truly be watching the Performers.

They may be watching **The Director**.

These concepts are deliberately unresolved.

## 18. Narrative Design Principle

The Theater functions on two levels.

### Surface Story

A chaotic cosmic showman recruits the player as Co-Director. Together
they summon a huge Cast of heroes, monsters, animals, and strange beings
and put on increasingly outrageous fantasy Productions.

### Deeper Story

Something about the Grand Theater is wrong.

The Director knows more than he says. The player was not chosen
randomly. The Cast may not have been gathered simply for entertainment.

Behind the comedy is a mystery involving the Theater, its Audience,
fate, stories, and why these individuals were brought together.

Reveal this gradually rather than overwhelming the opening.

## 20. Working Title

Current ideas:

-   **Stagebound**
-   **The Grand Stage**
-   **Fate's Encore**
-   **Final Encore**
-   **The Endless Stage**
-   **Fatebound: The Grand Stage**

### Current Strongest Candidate

# **Stagebound**

Possible tagline:

> **Every hero has a role to play.**

"Stagebound" works because Performers are literally bound to the Grand
Theater, their stories and fates become connected, and the title does
not lock the game exclusively to dice mechanics.

The final title remains undecided.

## 21. Core Creative Pillars

### 1. The Grand Theater Is a Place

Menus and mechanics should feel physically connected to the Theater
whenever practical.

### 2. The Cast Can Be Wildly Diverse

The interdimensional premise permits unusual characters without
requiring a shared culture or aesthetic.

### 3. Performance Is Part of Combat

Lights, curtains, audience reactions, scenery, entrances, exits, and
theatrical exaggeration should distinguish battles visually.

### 4. The Player Matters In-Universe

The player is the Co-Director, not an invisible UI operator and not The
Director.

### 5. Comedy Does Not Eliminate Stakes

The game can be funny without making its characters or story
meaningless.

### 6. The Theater Should Grow

Visual improvements to the Theater communicate long-term idle-game
progression.

### 7. Something Is Happening Behind the Curtain

The larger mystery should exist in small details long before it becomes
the story's focus.

## 22. One-Sentence Pitch

> **A cosmic trickster recruits you as Co-Director of an impossible
> theater between worlds, where you assemble a Cast of warriors,
> monsters, and unlikely heroes to battle through interdimensional
> Productions while uncovering why the Grand Theater gathered you all in
> the first place.**

## 23. Development Guardrail

The story exists to support the game, not constrain it.

New gameplay ideas should not be rejected simply because the current
lore does not explain them. The Grand Theater is deliberately flexible
enough to incorporate new worlds, characters, game modes, seasonal
events, enemies, and mechanics.

When introducing a new system, ask:

> **"How would The Grand Theater present this?"**

rather than:

> **"Are we allowed to do this because of the lore?"**

The Theater should make the game's possibilities larger, not smaller.
